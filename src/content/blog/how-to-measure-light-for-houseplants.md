"Bright indirect light" appears on almost every plant label and is almost never explained. It is also the instruction people most often get wrong, because human vision adapts to a huge range of brightness and plants do not.

## Your eyes are the problem

Walk from outside into a living room and it feels dim for a moment, then normal. Your pupils have adjusted, your visual system has recalibrated, and the room now looks "bright".

In actual light energy, that room might be delivering **less than 2% of what was falling on the pavement outside**. A plant experiences that difference directly, with no adaptation.

This is why "it's a really bright room" is not reliable information about whether a plant will thrive there.

## The three tests

### 1. The shadow test

The most useful method, and it costs nothing. On a bright day around midday, hold your hand about a foot above a sheet of white paper where you are thinking of putting the plant.

| Shadow | Light level | What grows there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sharp, dark, crisp edges | Direct sun | Succulents, cacti, aloe, citrus |
| Soft with fuzzy edges | Bright indirect | Monstera, fiddle leaf fig, most tropicals |
| Faint and blurry | Medium | Pothos, philodendron, peace lily, calathea |
| Barely visible | Low | Snake plant, ZZ plant, cast iron plant |
| None | Too dark | Nothing; add a grow light |

### 2. The reading test

Simpler still: at midday with no lamps on, could you comfortably read a paperback in that spot?

- **Easily, for an hour** → good light
- **Possible but you'd rather have a lamp** → medium
- **You would turn a lamp on** → low light, regardless of how bright it feels

### 3. A phone light meter

Free light meter apps give a rough lux reading. Phone sensors are not calibrated instruments, but they are useful for comparing spots in your own home.

| Lux (midday) | Level |
| --- | --- |
| 10,000+ | Direct sun |
| 2,000–10,000 | Bright indirect |
| 800–2,000 | Medium |
| 200–800 | Low |
| Under 200 | Too dark for most plants |

For reference, an overcast day outdoors is around 10,000 lux and full sun is over 100,000. That gap is why even a bright window is a fairly dim place from a plant's perspective.

## Window direction

In the northern hemisphere:

- **South-facing** — the brightest, direct sun for much of the day. Ideal for succulents, cacti and citrus; tropicals need a sheer curtain or a few feet of distance.
- **East-facing** — gentle direct sun in the morning, bright indirect the rest of the day. The best general-purpose window for houseplants.
- **West-facing** — indirect in the morning, intense direct sun in the afternoon. Hotter and harsher than east; watch for scorch.
- **North-facing** — no direct sun, steady indirect light all day. Good for ferns, calatheas and low-light tolerant plants.

Reverse north and south in the southern hemisphere.

Obstructions matter as much as direction. A south window behind a large tree or a balcony overhang may deliver less than an unobstructed north one.

## Distance is everything

This is the most underestimated factor. **Light intensity falls off roughly with the square of the distance from the window.**

Approximate proportions of the light available right at the glass:

| Distance from window | Light remaining |
| --- | --- |
| At the glass | 100% |
| 3 ft | ~50% |
| 6 ft | ~20% |
| 10 ft | ~5% |

A plant on a windowsill and the same plant across the room are in completely different environments, even though both spots look bright to you. Moving a struggling plant three feet closer to a window is often the entire fix.

## What "indirect" means

Indirect light is bright light that does not fall directly on the leaves as a beam of sun. Sources:

- Light from a large area of visible sky (not the sun itself)
- Sun filtered through a sheer curtain
- Sun bounced off a light-coloured wall

The practical test is whether the sun's disc is shining onto the leaves. If a beam falls directly on the foliage for more than an hour or so, that is direct light — fine for succulents, potentially scorching for tropicals.

## Reading light off the plant

Plants report their own light conditions:

**Too little:**
- Long bare stretches of stem between leaves — [legginess](/problems/leggy-stretched-houseplants/)
- New leaves smaller than old ones
- Variegation fading to plain green
- Growth stalled for months
- Leaning hard toward the window

**Too much:**
- Bleached, pale or tan patches on the leaves facing the light
- Leaves curling away from the source
- Crisp brown scorch marks, usually on the sunward side

## Seasonal change

Winter light in temperate regions can be **a fifth or less** of summer light, and the days are much shorter. Many plants that thrive in a spot in July are struggling by January.

Two adjustments help:

1. **Move plants closer to windows in winter**, and back once spring light returns.
2. **Add a grow light.** A basic LED on a timer for 10–12 hours a day costs very little to run and completely solves winter stretching. For succulents in northern climates it is close to essential.

## Matching plants to what you have

| Your spot | Good choices |
| --- | --- |
| Sunny south windowsill | Aloe, echeveria, cacti, citrus |
| Bright east window | Monstera, fiddle leaf fig, orchid, rubber plant |
| A few feet from a bright window | Philodendron, peace lily, spider plant |
| North window | Calathea, ferns, pothos |
| Dim corner of a lit room | Snake plant, ZZ plant, cast iron plant |
| Windowless room | A grow light, or nothing |

The most reliable way to succeed is to choose plants for the light you actually have, rather than buying the plant you want and hoping the corner will do.
