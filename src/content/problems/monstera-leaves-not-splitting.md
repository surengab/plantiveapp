A Monstera with no fenestrations is not automatically a sick plant. Start with its growth history: **has it never made a split leaf, or has a previously split plant begun producing solid leaves?** That distinction makes the checklist above more useful.

## Do Monstera leaves split as they grow?

There are two different kinds of growth here. The **plant** can produce increasingly divided leaves as it matures. A **fully opened leaf** does not acquire a new pattern of natural holes later. Tears from handling or damage are different from fenestrations.

Keep healthy solid leaves. They still contribute to the plant, and removing them does not make the next leaf split. The [University of Wisconsin Extension Monstera profile](https://hort.extension.wisc.edu/articles/monstera-deliciosa/) describes the difference between juvenile, solid foliage and the divided leaves of mature growth.

## Why does my Monstera not have fenestrations?

Use this comparison before buying supplies or changing several things at once.

| What you observe | What to check next |
| --- | --- |
| Only small, solid leaves so far | Juvenile growth; take a baseline photo and check the growing conditions |
| Older split leaves, newer solid leaves | A change in light, a new shoot, or other signs of stress |
| Long stem growing sideways | Whether the main stem has stable support |
| Solid leaves plus yellowing or wilting | Moisture and roots; leaf shape is not the only issue |

For a plant whose leaves are also yellowing, use the [overwatering versus underwatering checklist](/problems/overwatering-vs-underwatering/) before adding water or fertilizer.

## How to encourage new leaves to split

### Check light at the leaves

Bright indirect light is a useful starting point. The brightness of the room or a fixed distance from a window is not enough to describe the plant's actual conditions: window size, outdoor obstructions and season all matter. Use the [houseplant light guide](/blog/how-to-measure-light-for-houseplants/) to compare locations in your home.

The [RHS Monstera guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/swiss-cheese-plants) notes that very low light can mean fewer holes. Avoid a sudden move into intense direct sun.

### Give a climbing plant support

A stable support can help a sprawling stem grow upward. Secure the main stem loosely, leaving leaf stalks free to move. The [RHS growing guide](https://www.rhs.org.uk/plants/swiss-cheese-plants/how-to-grow-swiss-cheese-plants) describes how climbing plants often develop larger, more divided leaves, and how new growth after pruning can return to a juvenile form. A moss pole is an option, not a promise of a particular result.

### Check the identity and the care routine

Make sure you are comparing the same plant. A [heartleaf philodendron](/plant-care/heartleaf-philodendron/) is not a young Monstera, even though both can have heart-shaped leaves. Use a [clear leaf and whole-plant photo](/blog/how-to-identify-a-plant-from-a-photo/) if the label is uncertain.

Use the [Monstera deliciosa care guide](/plant-care/monstera-deliciosa/) for the routine. Do not repot a healthy plant or increase fertilizer solely because one leaf lacks holes.

## What to record over the next few leaves

Take a photo when each new leaf has fully opened. Record the date, approximate leaf width, number of edge splits and number of interior holes. Note any move, pruning or repotting beside the photo.

This is a comparison method, not a timetable: a slow-growing plant may need much longer than a few weeks to provide new evidence. If growth stops and other symptoms appear, investigate those symptoms instead of waiting for fenestration alone.
