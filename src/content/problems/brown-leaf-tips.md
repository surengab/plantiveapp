Brown crispy tips are the most misdiagnosed houseplant symptom. The instinct is to water more, and for most cases that is the wrong move — sometimes actively harmful.

## What brown tips actually are

The tip of a leaf is the end of the plumbing. Water travels from roots to leaf and evaporates from the surface; whatever was dissolved in that water — minerals, fertiliser salts, fluoride — stays behind, and it concentrates furthest from the source.

That is why tips go first. It is also why the three most common causes are all about **what is in the water and the air**, not how much water you give.

## Narrowing it down

| Clue | Likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| Several plants affected, worse in winter | Low humidity |
| Only sensitive species (spider plant, calathea, dracaena) | Tap water minerals |
| White crust on soil surface or pot rim | Over-fertilising |
| Plant droops badly between waterings | Inconsistent watering |
| Roots circling out of drainage holes | Root-bound |
| Damage on one side only | Draft or radiator |

## Low humidity

Most tropical houseplants evolved at 60–90% humidity. A heated home in winter runs at 25–35%. The plant loses water from its leaves faster than roots can supply it, and the tips desiccate.

**What works:**

- **A humidifier** near the plants. The only genuinely effective fix.
- **Grouping plants together** — collective transpiration raises local humidity measurably.
- **A pebble tray** — a shallow tray of stones and water under the pot. Modest but real.

**What does not work:** misting. The humidity spike lasts minutes, and wet foliage overnight invites fungal spotting.

## Tap water minerals

Some species are markedly sensitive to fluoride and hard-water minerals:

- Spider plants
- Calatheas and prayer plants
- Dracaenas, including corn plants and lucky bamboo
- Cordylines
- Peace lilies

For these, switch to **filtered, distilled or rainwater**. Note that leaving tap water out overnight only off-gasses chlorine — it does nothing about fluoride or dissolved hardness, which are the bigger culprits.

## Over-fertilising

Fertiliser is salt. Apply more than the plant uses and it accumulates in the soil, raising the concentration around the roots until they struggle to take up water at all — and the tips burn.

Look for a **white or yellowish crust** on the soil surface or around the drainage holes. That is the confirmation.

**To fix it:**

1. Take the pot to a sink and run plain water through the soil for two full minutes.
2. Let it drain completely.
3. Repeat monthly for two or three months.
4. Resume feeding at half or quarter strength, and less often than before.

Most houseplants need far less fertiliser than the bottle suggests. Half strength monthly during spring and summer, nothing in winter, suits the great majority.

## Inconsistent watering

Letting a plant dry out to the point of wilting, then soaking it, then repeating, damages the fine root hairs that do the actual absorbing. Even once you improve the routine, the reduced root capacity shows up as tip burn for a while.

The fix is rhythm, not volume: check the soil on the same day each week and water only if it is dry at the depth that plant wants.

## Should you trim them?

Cosmetically, yes. Use clean scissors and cut at a shallow angle that follows the leaf's natural taper, leaving a hair's width of brown. Cutting into green tissue creates a fresh wound that often browns again.

Just remember trimming is cosmetic. If you have not changed the water, humidity or feeding, the new tips will brown too.

## Judge by new leaves

As with most plant problems, the verdict comes from **new growth**. Existing brown tips never heal. If the leaves that unfurl over the following weeks come in clean and stay clean, you found the right cause.
