A leggy plant has long bare stretches of stem with leaves spaced far apart, usually leaning noticeably in one direction. It is one of the easiest problems to diagnose, because there is essentially one cause.

## It is the light

Plants have photoreceptors that measure both light quantity and quality. When light is insufficient, the plant redirects resources into stem elongation, a bet that growing taller will reach a better light source. The botanical term is **etiolation**.

The trade-off is that the plant spends its energy on stem rather than leaves, so you also get:

- Smaller new leaves than old ones
- Paler, thinner foliage
- Weak stems that flop
- Faded variegation and lost colour
- Stems leaning hard toward the window

## Why your bright room is not bright

The most common objection is "but it is right by a window" or "the room is really light." Two things are happening.

**Your eyes lie.** Human vision adapts across an enormous range, so a room that feels bright to you might be delivering a tiny fraction of outdoor light. A plant has no such adaptation.

**Light falls off fast.** Intensity drops roughly with the square of the distance from the window. A plant one metre from the glass may receive several times the light of one three metres back, even though both spots look similar.

A practical test: at midday, could you comfortably read a paperback there without turning a lamp on? If not, it is a low-light spot regardless of how it feels.

## Fixing it, in order

### 1. Improve the light

- Move the plant **closer to the window**, where a metre makes a real difference
- Choose an **east or south-facing** window where possible
- **Clean the glass** and open blinds fully
- **Rotate the pot** a quarter turn weekly so growth stays even
- Add an **LED grow light** on a timer for 10-12 hours a day if natural light is genuinely insufficient

Grow lights are the underrated answer. They are inexpensive to buy and run, and in a north-facing flat they are often the only thing that works.

### 2. Prune the stretched growth

This is the step people skip, and it is essential: **stretched growth never re-compacts.** The plant cannot shorten gaps it has already grown. Improving the light only affects what comes next.

**For vining plants** (pothos, philodendron, ivy): cut each long stem back by about a third, always just above a node. The plant branches from the cut, so one stem becomes two or three. Root the cuttings in water and plant them back into the bare centre of the pot.

**For upright plants** (rubber plant, fiddle leaf fig, dracaena): cut the main stem just above a node in spring. Two or three new branches typically emerge below the cut.

**For succulents** (echeveria, and most rosette types): behead it. Cut the rosette off with about an inch of stem, let the cut callus for three to five days, then set it on dry cactus mix to re-root. The old stump usually sprouts several offsets around the cut, so you gain plants.

### 3. Ease off the fertiliser

Excess nitrogen drives fast, soft, floppy growth. This is a particular trap with succulents, which need very little feeding and respond to a standard houseplant schedule with pale stretched growth.

Cut back to half strength, less frequently, and stop entirely in winter.

## When it is not a problem

Some plants simply grow this way. Rubber plants, fiddle leaf figs, dracaenas and most palms naturally form a single upright stem with foliage at the top. That is their habit, not a complaint.

The distinction: on a naturally upright plant, the leaves at the top are **large and well-coloured**. On a light-starved plant, new leaves are **smaller and paler** than the old ones.

If you want a branched shape from a naturally single-stemmed plant, prune the growing tip in spring. That works regardless of light.

## Preventing it

- Put high-light plants (succulents, ficus, citrus) in your brightest spots and accept that low-light corners need genuinely low-light species
- Rotate everything regularly
- Prune vining plants back each spring as routine maintenance, not as a rescue
- Add supplementary light in winter, when even a good window delivers a fraction of summer levels
