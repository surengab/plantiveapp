A new houseplant has just moved from a greenhouse to a shop, then through transport into your home. The kindest first-week plan is mostly observation. Do not respond to every imperfect leaf by changing the soil, adding fertilizer and moving the plant repeatedly.

## Before it joins your collection

Put the new plant in a bright spot away from your other plants for at least the first week; one to two weeks gives hidden pests more time to become visible.

Inspect it in good light:

- Look under leaves for webbing, pale stippling, insects or eggs.
- Check stem joints and leaf axils for cottony material or sticky residue.
- Look around the pot rim, drainage holes and saucer.
- Notice whether the soil smells sour or stays saturated.
- Remove dead leaves resting on the potting mix.

If you find pests, keep the plant isolated and identify the problem before treating it. Start with the relevant [plant problem guide](/problems/) rather than using a general pesticide on an unknown issue.

## Confirm what plant you bought

Shop labels are often broad or missing. Confirm the botanical name before choosing a permanent position because care can differ between plants sold under the same casual name.

Use several features—leaf shape, veins, stems, growth habit and flowers if present—rather than trusting one photo result. Plantive can suggest a species from a photo; compare that suggestion with a [species care guide](/plant-care/) before acting on it.

## Choose a stable first position

Match the plant to the light it needs, then leave it there long enough to adjust. Avoid a cold draft, a heating vent or leaves pressed against hot or cold glass.

Do not assume that a generally bright room provides bright light at the plant. Light falls quickly with distance from a window. If the new growth later becomes pale, small or stretched, use the [houseplant light guide](/blog/how-to-measure-light-for-houseplants/) to assess the position.

## Learn the moisture pattern

Check the potting mix when the plant arrives, but water only if the species and the actual moisture level say it is ready. A recently delivered plant may already have been watered.

Feel below the surface and lift the pot to learn its weight. When watering is needed, soak the full root ball, let water drain freely, and empty the saucer. Record the date. That gives you the first useful data point for this plant in this exact pot and position.

## Wait before making big changes

Unless there is a clear problem, skip these during the first week:

- **Routine repotting.** A decorative nursery pot is not an emergency if it drains and the roots are healthy.
- **Fertilizer.** It cannot fix transport stress, low light or damaged roots.
- **Pruning healthy growth.** Remove only dead or badly damaged material while the plant settles.
- **Daily moves.** Constantly changing light and temperature makes the plant harder to read.

One or two older leaves may yellow after a move. Watch the trend. Rapid decline, a wet pot that never dries, spreading spots or visible pests deserve investigation; one cosmetic flaw does not.

## Your first-week checklist

1. Photograph the plant and its label.
2. Inspect leaves, stems, soil and pot.
3. Keep it separate from the collection.
4. Confirm the species and its light needs.
5. Check moisture instead of watering automatically.
6. Leave it in one suitable position.
7. Recheck for pests twice during the week.

At the end of the week, you should know more about how the plant behaves in your home. That is a better basis for care than trying to perfect everything on day one.
