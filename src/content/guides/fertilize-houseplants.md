Fertilizer supplies nutrients a plant uses to build new tissue. It is not medicine. If a plant is declining because the roots are wet, the room is dark or pests are feeding on it, fertilizer adds another variable without correcting the cause.

## Feed growth, not the calendar

Start when the plant is producing healthy new leaves or shoots. For many houseplants in the Northern Hemisphere, active growth increases in spring and continues through early autumn. Plants under consistent grow lights may follow a different rhythm.

Reduce or stop feeding when growth pauses during short winter days. Also wait when a plant is newly purchased, badly wilted, pest-stressed or recovering from root damage.

## Choose a simple fertilizer

A balanced, water-soluble houseplant fertilizer is enough for most foliage plants. Slow-release products also work, but they offer less control if the plant's growth slows.

Read the label first. The nutrient ratio matters less than applying the product safely and consistently. More is not better: excess fertilizer can build soluble salts in the potting mix and damage root tips.

## Start below full strength

General-purpose fertilizer labels are often written for faster-growing outdoor plants. University extension guidance commonly recommends starting indoor plants at half or even quarter label strength.

A safe routine is:

1. Confirm that the plant is actively growing.
2. Make sure the potting mix is not bone dry.
3. Dilute the fertilizer according to the houseplant rate—or start weaker if it is a general product.
4. Apply evenly to the potting mix, never exceeding the label.
5. Watch the next two or three weeks of growth before repeating.

Species, light and growth rate matter. A large plant in strong light uses nutrients faster than a slow plant in low light.

## After repotting

Fresh commercial potting mix often contains starter fertilizer. Read the bag before adding more. Waiting at least two to four weeks after repotting avoids stacking fertilizer on top of what is already present and gives disturbed roots time to recover.

See the [repotting guide](/blog/how-to-repot-a-houseplant/) for signs that a plant genuinely needs a larger container.

## Signs you may be overfeeding

Watch for:

- A white crust on the soil, pot rim or saucer
- Brown leaf tips appearing after feeding
- Wilting even though the potting mix is moist
- Weak, stretched growth that the available light cannot support
- Damaged root tips or stalled growth

These signs overlap with other problems, so check watering, light and root health too. If salts have accumulated, pause fertilizer and flush the potting mix thoroughly with plain water, allowing it to drain freely.

## When fertilizer is not the answer

Do not reach for fertilizer first when a plant has:

- Persistently wet soil or a sour smell
- Sudden widespread yellowing
- Visible insects, webbing or sticky residue
- No usable light
- Severe drought damage

Start with the symptom in the [plant problem library](/problems/). Once the primary problem is corrected and healthy growth resumes, modest feeding can support recovery.

The useful rule is simple: fertilizer follows healthy growth. It does not create the conditions healthy growth requires.
