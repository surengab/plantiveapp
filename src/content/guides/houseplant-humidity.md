Many tropical plants tolerate ordinary household air. A smaller group—ferns, calatheas, carnivorous plants and some thin-leaved species—shows stress when indoor air becomes very dry, especially during winter heating.

Before adding equipment, confirm that humidity is the real problem.

## Measure the room first

A small digital hygrometer gives you a far better answer than guessing from one brown leaf tip. Measure near the plants for several days, including while heating or air conditioning is running.

Brown tips alone do not prove low humidity. They can also come from inconsistent watering, fertilizer salts, damaged roots or sensitive plants receiving hard water. Use the [brown leaf tips guide](/problems/brown-leaf-tips/) to compare the other clues.

## What actually raises humidity

### A room humidifier

A humidifier provides the most controllable, lasting increase. Place it close enough to affect the plants but not so close that foliage and furniture remain wet. Clean the unit on its recommended schedule to prevent mineral and microbial buildup.

Use a hygrometer rather than running it continuously. Excessively damp rooms can encourage condensation and mould.

### Grouping compatible plants

Plants release some moisture through transpiration, so a group can create a slightly more humid pocket than isolated pots. Group plants only when they have similar light and temperature needs, and leave enough space for air to move between leaves.

Grouping also makes pests easier to spread. Keep new or affected plants separate.

### A pebble tray

A shallow tray of water and pebbles may create a small local effect as water evaporates. The pot must sit on the pebbles above the water, not in it. Treat this as a modest supplement, not a substitute for a humidifier in a very dry room.

### A naturally humid room

A bright bathroom can suit humidity-loving plants if it also provides enough light. Humidity without usable light is not an improvement.

## Why misting is not the solution

Misting changes humidity for only a few minutes. It is too brief to recreate a humid growing environment, and repeatedly wet foliage can help some diseases spread.

Misting can loosen dust before cleaning, but rinse or wipe intentionally and let the foliage dry. Follow the [leaf-cleaning guide](/guides/clean-houseplant-leaves/) rather than using mist as daily humidity care.

## Keep airflow in the plan

Humidity and still, wet air are not the same thing. Plants benefit from gentle air movement, especially when grouped. Do not press foliage against cold windows or pack pots so closely that wet leaves cannot dry.

## Sometimes the right answer is a different plant

If a room stays dry and a humidifier is impractical, choose species that tolerate it. Snake plants, ZZ plants, many succulents, pothos and heartleaf philodendron are generally less demanding than thin-leaved ferns or calatheas.

Start with the environment you actually have. Matching the plant to the room is simpler and more reliable than trying to turn an entire home into a greenhouse.
