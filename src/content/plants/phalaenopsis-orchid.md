Most supermarket orchids die within a year, and almost always for the same reason: they were treated like a normal houseplant in a pot of soil, watered on a schedule with an ice cube. Phalaenopsis are epiphytes. Understand that one fact and the rest of their care follows logically.

## They grow on trees, not in soil

In the wild a Phalaenopsis clings to a tree trunk in dappled shade, its thick roots exposed to air, drenched by rain and then dried by breeze within hours.

Everything about their care recreates that cycle:

- **Coarse bark, not soil.** Bark provides the air gaps roots need. Soil suffocates them.
- **Clear plastic pot.** Lets you see root colour, which is your watering gauge. Slip it inside a decorative cachepot for looks.
- **Drench, then dry.** Not a little water often.

## Watering by root colour

This is the single most useful skill for keeping orchids alive, and the clear pot makes it easy.

| Root colour | Meaning | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bright green | Freshly watered, fully hydrated | Wait |
| Pale green | Drying down | Wait |
| Silvery-grey | Thirsty | Water now |
| Brown, soft, mushy | Rotted | Repot and trim immediately |
| Brown, flat, papery | Dead and dried | Trim at next repot |

To water: take the pot to the sink and run tepid water through the bark for about a minute so everything is soaked. Let it drain completely. Tip out any water caught in the crown where the leaves meet, because water sitting there overnight causes crown rot, which is usually fatal.

Do **not** use the ice cube method. It is convenient and widely repeated, but cold water shocks tropical roots and a cube provides far too little water to soak the bark.

## Light

Bright but indirect. An east-facing windowsill with gentle morning sun is close to ideal.

Read the leaves to check:

- **Medium, grassy green**: light is right
- **Dark, deep green**: too little light; this is the most common reason orchids never rebloom
- **Yellowish-green or with reddish tinges**: a bit too much light
- **White or tan patches**: sunburn; move it out of direct sun

## Getting it to rebloom

This is where most people give up, and it is more mechanical than it seems. Phalaenopsis need two things:

1. **Enough light year-round.** A dark-green-leaved orchid will not spike no matter what else you do.
2. **A night-time temperature drop in autumn.** Four to six weeks with nights around 60°F (15°C) and days 10-15°F warmer triggers spike initiation.

In practice, leaving the orchid on a windowsill away from radiators through autumn provides that drop naturally. A new spike usually emerges within one to two months, and flowers open several weeks after that.

Feed weakly and regularly through the growing season, using a quarter-strength orchid fertiliser at most waterings, and flush with plain water monthly to prevent salt build-up in the bark.

## What to do when flowers drop

The blooms last two to four months, which is remarkable value. When they fall:

- **Spike fully brown and dry:** cut it off at the base with sterilised scissors. The plant will produce a fresh spike next season.
- **Spike still green:** cut just above the second node from the bottom. Phalaenopsis frequently branch from that node and flower again within a few months.

Keep watering and feeding as normal. The plant is not dormant, it is growing roots and leaves.

## Repotting

Repot every one to two years, after flowering, when the bark has broken down into fine dark crumbs. Old decomposed bark holds water like soil and is a leading cause of root rot in orchids that were fine for two years and then suddenly declined.

1. Slide the plant out and shake off the old bark.
2. Trim every brown, soft or papery root with sterilised scissors. Keep anything firm.
3. Settle it into a pot just big enough for the root mass, with fresh coarse bark packed loosely around.
4. Wait a few days before watering so cut surfaces can seal.

## Keikis

Occasionally a Phalaenopsis produces a **keiki**, a baby plant on an old flower spike, complete with its own leaves and roots. Leave it attached until it has two or three roots at least two inches long, then cut the spike either side of it and pot it into fine bark. It is a genetic clone of the parent.
