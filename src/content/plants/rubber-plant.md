The rubber plant is the fiddle leaf fig's easy-going relative. Same architectural presence, same big glossy leaves, considerably more tolerance for imperfect conditions. If a fiddle leaf fig has defeated you, this is the plant to try next.

## Watering

Water thoroughly when the top two inches of soil are dry — usually every 7 to 14 days in spring and summer, and every two to three weeks in winter.

Rubber plants have a wider margin than most ficus. They handle drying out further than a fiddle leaf fig would tolerate, and they signal thirst by letting the leaves droop slightly. What they do not tolerate is sitting in wet soil, which shows up as yellowing lower leaves that drop.

Pour until water runs from the drainage holes, then let it drain fully.

## Light and leaf colour

Bright indirect light is the sweet spot. It keeps the internodes short so the plant stays compact rather than stretching, and — for the coloured cultivars — it develops the pigment.

Varieties like **'Ruby'**, **'Burgundy'**, **'Tineke'** and **'Abidjan'** are sold for their red, cream and near-black tones. Those pigments are light-dependent. Put a Ruby in a dim corner and within a few months it will be producing ordinary green leaves.

Some gentle direct morning sun is beneficial. Harsh afternoon sun through glass will scorch the leaves.

## Leaf drop

Ficus species are dramatic about change, and rubber plants are no exception. Common triggers:

- Moving to a different room
- Cold air from a door or single-glazed window
- A radiator or heating vent starting up for the season
- Repotting
- Sharp changes in watering frequency

The response is the same in every case: shed some leaves, then adapt. Pick a stable spot away from vents and drafts, and leave the plant there. Losing a few leaves after a move is normal; losing them continuously for months means something in the environment is still wrong.

## Pruning for shape

A rubber plant naturally grows as a single upright stem. If you want a fuller, branched shape, prune it.

1. In spring, choose where you want branching to start.
2. Cut the main stem just above a leaf node with clean, sharp secateurs.
3. **Wear gloves.** The white latex that flows out irritates skin and stains fabric.
4. Blot the cut with a damp cloth until the sap stops.
5. Within a few weeks, two or three new shoots emerge below the cut.

Save the top section as a cutting — it roots readily.

## Propagation

**Stem cuttings.** Take a section with two or three leaves. Blot the sap, remove the lowest leaf, and root either in water or directly in damp potting mix. Covering the cutting loosely with a clear bag raises humidity and speeds rooting. Expect roots in four to eight weeks.

**Air layering.** More reliable for thick, woody stems. Cut a shallow upward notch a third into the stem, wedge it open with a matchstick, wrap the area in damp sphagnum moss, and seal with clear plastic. Roots form in the moss over one to two months, after which you cut below the root ball and pot it up.

## Keeping the leaves clean

Those big leaves are dust magnets, and dust genuinely reduces the light reaching the leaf surface. Wipe both sides with a damp cloth every month, supporting the leaf with your other hand.

Avoid commercial leaf-shine sprays. They block stomata and leave a film that attracts more dust than they remove.
