If you have killed houseplants before, start here. Pothos tolerates low light, irregular watering, dry air and neglect, and it tells you clearly when it is thirsty by drooping dramatically and then bouncing back within hours of a drink.

## Watering without a schedule

The rule for pothos is simple: **let the top half of the pot dry out**, then water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes.

Unlike most houseplants, pothos gives you an obvious visual signal. When the leaves go slightly soft and the vines droop, it wants water. Water it and it recovers within a few hours. Using that signal deliberately, waiting for the very first hint of droop rather than watering on a calendar, is the easiest way to never overwater it.

Expect roughly every 7-14 days in summer and every 2-3 weeks in winter, but always check first.

## Light and variegation

Pothos is famous for tolerating low light, and that is true, but with a catch. **Variegation is light-dependent.** The cream, gold and white markings on Marble Queen, Golden and Snow Queen pothos are areas with less chlorophyll. In dim conditions the plant compensates by producing greener leaves that photosynthesise more efficiently.

- **Plain green types** (Jade, Neon): happy several metres from a window
- **Variegated types** (Golden, Marble Queen, Manjula): need bright indirect light to hold their pattern
- **Highly variegated types** (Snow Queen): need the brightest indirect light you have

If your Marble Queen is coming in solid green, move it brighter and cut the reverted vine back to the last well-marked leaf.

## Making a pothos look full

A pothos left alone becomes a few very long vines with bare stems and leaves only at the ends. The fix is pruning, and it works quickly.

1. In spring, cut every long vine back by about a third, always just above a node.
2. The plant branches from each cut, doubling the number of growing tips.
3. Take the cuttings, root them in water for two weeks, and plant them back into the gaps in the same pot.

Within a season you have a dense, full plant instead of a stringy one.

## Propagation

Pothos is the plant people learn propagation on because it barely ever fails.

- Cut a section of vine with **at least one node** and 2-4 leaves.
- Strip the leaf closest to the cut so the node sits bare.
- Stand it in a glass of water with the node submerged.
- Change the water weekly. Roots appear in 7-14 days.
- Pot into soil when roots reach 2 inches, or leave it in water indefinitely, since pothos grows in water permanently if you add a drop of liquid fertiliser monthly.

## Common problems

**Yellow leaves.** Almost always overwatering. Feel the soil; if it is damp, you have your answer. One yellow leaf at the very base of an old vine is just ageing.

**Black stem bases and a sour smell.** Root rot. Unpot, cut away every mushy brown root, and repot into fresh dry mix in a smaller pot. Take healthy cuttings as insurance.

**Fine webbing under leaves.** Spider mites, encouraged by dry air. Shower the plant, then treat with insecticidal soap weekly for three weeks.

**Sticky residue on leaves or the shelf below.** Scale or mealybugs. Dab each insect with a cotton bud dipped in rubbing alcohol.

## Pothos and pets

This matters and is often glossed over. Pothos is toxic to cats and dogs, because the calcium oxalate crystals cause immediate, intense oral pain. It is not usually fatal, but it is genuinely distressing for the animal. Because pothos is so often grown as a trailing plant, hanging it does not always help; cats will chase a dangling vine.

If you have a determined chewer, choose a [spider plant](/plant-care/spider-plant/) instead. It is non-toxic and grows in similar conditions.
