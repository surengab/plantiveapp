The heartleaf philodendron is the plant most often mistaken for a pothos, and it is arguably the better of the two — softer leaves, faster growth, a little more tolerance of dim rooms. It has been a standard houseplant for over a century because it is genuinely hard to fail with.

## Telling it apart from pothos

They are sold side by side and constantly confused. The reliable differences:

| Feature | Heartleaf philodendron | Pothos |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Leaf texture | Thin, soft, matte | Thick, waxy, glossy |
| Leaf shape | Symmetrical heart | Slightly asymmetric |
| New growth | Emerges from a papery sheath (cataphyll) | Unfurls from the previous leaf |
| Aerial roots | Thin, several per node | One thick nub per node |
| Petiole | Round | Grooved on top |

If you can see a dried papery sheath left behind next to a new leaf, it is a philodendron.

## Watering

Water when the top inch of soil is dry — every 7 to 10 days in the growing season, every 10 to 14 in winter.

Because the leaves are thinner than a pothos's, this plant wilts sooner and more visibly when thirsty. That is a useful signal, but do not lean on it: repeated severe wilting damages fine roots and causes permanent leaf-edge browning.

Water thoroughly until it runs from the drainage holes, and never leave the pot standing in water.

## Light

Medium to bright indirect light. It handles dimmer conditions than most trailing plants, but there is a real trade-off:

- **Bright indirect:** compact, dense growth with large leaves
- **Medium:** healthy but slower, leaves slightly smaller
- **Low:** survives, but vines stretch badly and leaves shrink

Keep it out of direct sun entirely — the thin leaves scorch and bleach faster than a pothos would.

## Keeping it full

The default failure mode is not death, it is a plant with four long bare vines and a bald centre. Fix it the same way every year:

1. **Prune in spring.** Cut each vine back by about a third, just above a node. The plant branches from every cut.
2. **Root the cuttings.** Stand them in water for two weeks.
3. **Plant them back.** Tuck the rooted cuttings into the bare middle of the same pot.
4. **Move it brighter.** Stretching is a light problem at heart.

Do this each spring and the plant gets denser every year instead of stringier.

## Propagation

Almost as easy as pothos:

- Cut a vine section with at least one node and two or three leaves
- Remove the leaf nearest the cut so the node sits bare
- Stand it in water with the node submerged, leaves above the surface
- Change the water weekly; roots appear in 7–14 days
- Pot into airy mix once roots reach an inch or two

Cuttings can also be rooted directly in damp soil, which skips the water-to-soil transition and often produces sturdier plants — just keep the mix consistently moist for the first few weeks.

## Climbing for bigger leaves

Like its relative the Monstera, a heartleaf philodendron produces larger leaves when it climbs rather than trails. Give it a moss pole or let it grow up a trellis and the difference in leaf size within a year is substantial.

If you prefer the trailing look, that is fine — just accept the smaller leaves as part of the trade.
