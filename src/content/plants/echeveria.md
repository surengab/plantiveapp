Echeverias are the rosette succulents that fill Pinterest boards — powder-blue, dusty pink, edged in red. They are easy in the sense that they need very little from you, and difficult in the sense that most homes do not have enough light to keep them looking the way they do in the photographs.

## Light is the whole story

An echeveria needs **six or more hours of direct sun**. Not bright indirect — direct.

Given that, it stays tight and compact, and produces the pink, purple and blue tones it is grown for. Given less, two things happen:

1. **It stretches.** The stem elongates and the leaves space out, reaching for light. This is called etiolation, and it is permanent — stretched growth never re-compacts.
2. **It fades to green.** The colours are anthocyanins, stress pigments the plant makes under strong sun. Comfortable plants stop making them.

A south-facing windowsill is the best spot in most homes. If you do not have one, an inexpensive LED grow light on a timer for 10–12 hours a day genuinely solves the problem.

## Fixing a stretched rosette

You cannot undo etiolation, but you can restart the plant. This is standard practice and echeverias take it well.

1. Cut the rosette off with about an inch of stem attached, using a clean sharp blade.
2. Strip the lowest few leaves from that stem — save them, they propagate.
3. Leave the cutting in open air for **three to five days** to callus over.
4. Set it on dry cactus mix. Roots grow down into the soil within two or three weeks.
5. Wait until roots have formed before watering properly.

The original stump usually sprouts several offsets around the cut, so you end up with more plants than you started with.

## Watering

Soak thoroughly, then leave it alone until the soil is completely dry — every two to three weeks in the growing season, monthly or less in winter.

Two rules matter more than frequency:

- **Water the soil, not the plant.** Water pooling in the centre of the rosette rots the crown, and crown rot is usually fatal. Pour at the soil surface, or bottom-water by standing the pot in a shallow tray for 15 minutes.
- **Read the leaves.** Wrinkled and curling down means thirsty. Translucent, yellow and squishy means overwatered — stop immediately.

## Pot and soil

Shallow terracotta, barely wider than the rosette, with a drainage hole. Terracotta breathes; a snug pot dries out fast; a drainage hole is non-negotiable.

For mix, cut cactus soil with 30–50% pumice, perlite or coarse grit. Damp mix should crumble apart rather than hold a shape when squeezed. Those decorative arrangements sold in glass bowls without drainage are essentially a countdown.

## Propagating from leaves

This is the most satisfying thing echeverias do.

1. Choose a plump, healthy leaf from low on the rosette.
2. Grip it near the stem and **twist gently sideways** until it pops off cleanly. The whole base must come away — a leaf torn across will not root.
3. Lay it on dry cactus mix, bright light but out of direct sun.
4. Leave it for three to five days to callus.
5. Mist lightly every few days.

Roots appear in two to three weeks, then a tiny rosette forms at the base. The parent leaf shrivels as the baby consumes it — that is the process working, not a failure. Once the new rosette is thumbnail-sized and rooted, pot it up properly.

Not every leaf takes. Start six and expect four.

## Winter

Echeverias slow right down in winter. Water sparingly — once a month is often plenty — and keep them in the brightest, coolest spot you have. Cool bright winters with lean water actually produce the best colour of the year.

Watch for stretching in the low winter light. If new growth is spacing out, that is the signal to add a grow light rather than wait for spring.
