If the snake plant is hard to kill, the ZZ plant is harder. It stores water underground in thick rhizomes, tolerates deep shade, ignores humidity, and looks glossy and deliberate the entire time. It is the plant for the hallway with one small window, or the office desk you leave for two weeks at a time.

## Watering: less than you think

The whole care routine hinges on this. Those swollen rhizomes under the soil are water tanks — the plant is built for a climate with long dry seasons.

**Let the soil dry out completely, then water thoroughly.** Every three to six weeks in the growing season is typical. In a dim, cool room in winter, once every eight weeks is entirely reasonable.

If you are unsure, wait. A thirsty ZZ plant shows no symptoms for weeks. An overwatered one rots from the rhizome outward and often looks fine right up until several stems collapse at once.

## Light

ZZ plants photosynthesise efficiently enough to grow in genuinely low light — a bookshelf across the room from a window, a north-facing hall, an office lit by ceiling panels.

That said, "tolerates low light" means slow growth. In bright indirect light a ZZ plant might push three or four new stems a year; in a dim corner, one. Both plants stay healthy.

The one thing to avoid is direct summer sun, which scorches the glossy leaflets.

## Recognising rhizome rot

The failure mode is always the same, and it starts underground where you cannot see it.

Warning signs, roughly in order:

1. Soil that is still damp three weeks after watering
2. One or two stems yellowing from the bottom up
3. Stem bases that feel soft when squeezed gently
4. Stems falling away from the plant with no resistance

To treat it, tip the plant out and inspect the rhizomes. Healthy ones are firm and pale, like a small potato. Cut away anything brown, soft or foul-smelling with a clean knife, let the cuts dry in open air for 24 hours, and repot into **dry** cactus mix. Wait a full week before the first watering.

## Propagation

**Division** is the fast route. In spring, tip the plant out, tease the rhizome clumps apart, and pot each piece with at least one stem into its own container. You get a mature plant immediately.

**Leaf cuttings** are the slow route, but they are strangely satisfying. Pull a leaflet from a stem, keeping the little base intact, and push it a centimetre into barely damp cactus mix. Over the next six to twelve months it forms a tiny rhizome, and eventually a shoot. Do several at once — some fail, and none of them are quick.

## Cleaning and upkeep

Those glossy leaflets collect dust, which dulls the finish and blocks light. Wipe them with a damp cloth every month or two. Skip leaf-shine products — they clog the pores the plant breathes through and leave a residue that attracts more dust.

Cut any stem that has gone yellow or become badly floppy right back to soil level. New stems emerge from the rhizome, not from the old stem, so there is nothing to save.
