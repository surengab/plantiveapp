Aloe vera fails in exactly two ways: not enough sun, and too much water. Get those right and it will sit on a windowsill producing pups for a decade.

## Light comes first

This is the part most people underestimate. Aloe is a desert plant that wants **six or more hours of direct sunlight**. A south or west-facing windowsill is ideal.

You can read the light situation off the plant itself:

| What the plant looks like | What it means |
| --- | --- |
| Thick, upright, plump leaves | Good light |
| Leaves flattening and splaying outward | Not enough light |
| Long pale gaps between leaves, stretching sideways | Well short of enough light |
| Bronze or reddish blush, still plump | Strong sun, usually fine |
| Bleached white patches | Sudden move into intense sun; introduce gradually |

Leaves that have already flattened will not stand back up. Move the plant to better light and judge by the new growth from the centre.

## Watering

Soak thoroughly, then leave it entirely alone until the soil is dry all the way through.

- **Spring and summer:** every 2-4 weeks
- **Winter:** every 5-6 weeks

When you water, take it to the sink and pour until water runs freely from the drainage holes. Let it drain completely, with no saucer of standing water. That deep-then-dry cycle mimics desert rainfall and is what the roots are adapted to.

**Reading the leaves:**

- Thin, curled, wrinkled leaves → thirsty, water it
- Soft, translucent, mushy leaves → overwatered, stop immediately

Those two look nothing alike once you have seen both. Wrinkled is dry; squishy is wet.

## Pot and soil

Use a **wide, shallow terracotta pot** with drainage holes. Aloe roots spread sideways rather than down, and porous terracotta pulls moisture out of the mix between waterings.

For soil, use a gritty cactus mix, or make your own with roughly two parts cactus mix to one part perlite or pumice. If you can squeeze a handful of damp mix and it holds together in a ball, it is too dense.

## Harvesting gel safely

If you are growing aloe to use it, harvest correctly:

1. Choose a **thick outer leaf**, the oldest and closest to the ground.
2. Cut it at the base with a clean knife.
3. Stand it cut-end down in a glass for about ten minutes. A yellow-brown liquid drains out. This is the **latex**, and it is the irritant. Discard it.
4. Rinse the leaf, lay it flat, slice off the spiny edges, and fillet off the top skin.
5. Scoop the clear gel.

Use it fresh on minor burns, sunburn and dry skin. Store leftovers in the fridge for a few days at most. Do not use it on deep wounds, burns that blister badly, or anything infected, and do not eat raw aloe gel casually; the latex is a strong laxative.

## Pups

A healthy aloe produces offsets around its base. Once a pup is about a third the size of the parent and has its own roots:

1. Tip the whole plant out of its pot.
2. Work the pup free, keeping as many of its roots as possible. Use a clean knife if it is firmly attached.
3. Leave the pup in open air for **two days** so cut surfaces callus over. Skipping this is how pups rot.
4. Pot into dry cactus mix.
5. Wait a further week before the first light watering.

Removing pups also helps the parent, which otherwise spends energy supporting a crowded clump.
