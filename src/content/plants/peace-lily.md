The peace lily is the most communicative houseplant there is. It wilts theatrically when thirsty, stands back up within hours of a drink, and flowers indoors more readily than almost anything else. That feedback loop makes it genuinely easy to learn on, as long as you understand what it is actually telling you.

## The drooping signal

A wilting peace lily looks like a disaster and usually is not. When the soil dries, the leaves lose turgor and the whole plant collapses. Water it thoroughly and it stands back up within two to four hours.

**Before you water, feel the soil.** This is the critical step:

- **Soil dry, plant drooping** → thirst. Water thoroughly.
- **Soil wet, plant drooping** → root rot. Watering again makes it worse.

The second case is the one that catches people out. A rotting root system cannot take up water, so the plant wilts exactly as if it were dry. If the soil is damp and the plant is limp, unpot it and check the roots. Healthy ones are firm and white; rotted ones are brown, soft and smell sour.

One caution: while a peace lily recovers from wilting, doing it repeatedly damages the fine root hairs and causes the leaf edges to brown permanently. Use the droop as a backstop, not as your watering schedule.

## Watering properly

Peace lilies like their soil **lightly and evenly moist**, making it one of the few common houseplants that does not want to dry out between waterings.

Check every few days by pushing a finger an inch into the soil. If it feels barely damp, water. Pour slowly until water runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer completely.

Water quality matters more here than for most plants. Peace lilies are sensitive to fluoride and chlorine, which accumulate at the leaf tips and burn them brown. Filtered water, rainwater, or tap water left in an open jug overnight all work.

## Getting it to flower

Those white "flowers" are technically spathes, modified leaves wrapping a spadix of tiny true flowers. To get them:

- **Light is the main lever.** Bright indirect light produces blooms; dim light produces leaves only. An east-facing window a few feet back is ideal.
- **Feed during the growing season.** A balanced fertiliser at quarter to half strength every six weeks from spring through summer.
- **Be patient after dividing.** A freshly split plant spends its first year rebuilding roots.
- **Do not repot too often.** A slightly snug pot encourages flowering.

Blooms last several weeks, fade from white to green, and eventually brown. Cut the whole stalk right at the base once it fades.

## Humidity and brown tips

Brown, crispy tips are the peace lily's most common cosmetic complaint. In order of likelihood:

1. **Dry air**, especially in winter with heating on. Aim for 50% humidity; a humidifier or a tray of pebbles and water near the plant helps. Misting does very little.
2. **Tap water chemicals.** Switch to filtered or stood water.
3. **Over-fertilising.** Reduce to quarter strength and flush the pot with plain water twice a year to leach out salts.

You can trim brown tips with clean scissors, cutting at an angle to follow the leaf's natural shape. It will not spread, but the trimmed edge will not regrow green either.

## Dividing

Peace lilies grow as clumping crowns and are easy to split. In spring, tip the plant out and look for natural divisions, meaning groups of leaves with their own roots. Pull or cut them apart, making sure each division has a healthy root mass and at least three or four leaves, and pot each into fresh moisture-retentive mix.

Keep divisions slightly more humid and out of direct light for their first few weeks while they establish.
