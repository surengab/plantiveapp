The fiddle leaf fig has a reputation for being difficult, and it is partly earned. It is not fragile so much as **specific**: it wants a lot of light, consistent watering, and to be left alone in one place. Meet those three conditions and it is straightforward. Miss them and it complains loudly, in brown spots.

## Reading brown spots correctly

This is the single most useful skill for keeping a fiddle leaf fig alive, because the two main causes need opposite treatments.

| What you see | Where it appears | Cause | What to do |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dark brown, sometimes with yellow halo | Centre and base of leaf, lower leaves first | Root rot from overwatering | Inspect roots, remove rotted material, water less |
| Dry, crispy, tan | Edges and tips, spreading inward | Underwatering, low humidity | Water more thoroughly, raise humidity |
| Pale, bleached, papery | Leaves facing the window | Sunburn | Filter direct afternoon sun |
| Small dark spots with yellow rings | Scattered anywhere | Bacterial or fungal leaf spot | Remove affected leaves, improve airflow, avoid wetting foliage |

If you take one thing from this guide: **dark spots from the middle mean too much water; crispy edges mean too little.**

## Light

Fiddle leaf figs come from the understorey and canopy edges of West African rainforest, where light is bright and abundant. Indoors they need the brightest spot you have.

- **Best:** 2–5 ft from an east-facing window, with gentle direct morning sun
- **Good:** near a south or west window, filtered through a sheer curtain
- **Marginal:** north window with clear sky view
- **Not enough:** interior walls, corners, anywhere more than 8 ft from a window

Rotate the plant a quarter turn every week or two. They lean toward light noticeably, and rotating keeps growth even.

## Watering rhythm

Consistency is the theme. Pick a day, check the soil, and water only if the top two inches are dry.

When you water, water **fully** — pour slowly until water runs freely from the drainage holes, which ensures the entire root ball is wetted. Then let it drain completely. Never leave the pot standing in a full saucer.

A useful trick is bottom-watering once a month: stand the pot in a few inches of water for 20–30 minutes so the soil draws moisture up evenly, then drain. This prevents the dry pockets that form when water channels down the sides of a root-bound pot.

## Stability matters more than you think

Fiddle leaf figs drop leaves in response to change. Common triggers:

- Moving the plant between rooms
- A door or window that lets in cold winter air
- A radiator or heating vent switching on for the season
- Air-conditioning blowing directly on the foliage
- Repotting

Choose the position carefully once, then leave it. A plant that drops a few leaves after a move is not dying — it is adjusting, and it will stop once conditions hold steady for a few weeks.

## Pruning and shaping

Left alone, a fiddle leaf fig grows as a single tall stem with leaves only near the top. To get the branched, tree-like shape:

**Pruning.** In spring, cut the main stem just above a node, removing 6–12 inches. Within a few weeks two or three new branches usually emerge below the cut. Use clean shears and wear gloves — the milky sap irritates skin.

**Notching.** If you do not want to lose height, cut a shallow wedge about a third of the way into the trunk, just above a node. This interrupts the hormone flow that suppresses side branching and often triggers a new branch at that point.

## Feeding and repotting

Feed monthly from spring to early autumn with a high-nitrogen fertiliser at half strength. Nitrogen drives the large leaf growth this plant is grown for.

Repot every two to three years in spring, moving up just one pot size. Expect the plant to pause for a month afterwards — that is normal, not a problem. In years when you do not repot, scrape off the top two inches of soil and replace it with fresh mix.
