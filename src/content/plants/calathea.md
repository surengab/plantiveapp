Calatheas are the most beautiful difficult plants in common cultivation. The foliage is genuinely spectacular — brushstroke patterns, purple undersides, leaves that rise and fall with the light. They are also unusually specific about humidity and water quality, and they will not quietly tolerate getting either wrong.

Worth knowing before you commit: what most shops sell as *Calathea* was reclassified into the genus *Goeppertia* in 2012. The care is unchanged; only the label moved.

## Humidity is the whole game

Calatheas come from the rainforest floor, where humidity sits between 70% and 90%. A typical heated home in winter runs at 25–35%.

That gap is why calathea leaves crisp at the edges, and no amount of careful watering compensates for it.

**What actually works:**

- **A humidifier** running near the plant. This is the only reliable solution.
- **Grouping plants together**, which raises local humidity meaningfully.
- **A pebble tray** — a shallow tray of stones and water under the pot. Modest effect, but real.
- **A bathroom with a window**, if you have one with enough light.

**What does not work:** misting. It raises humidity for a few minutes, and wet leaves left standing overnight actually encourage fungal spotting.

## Water quality

Calatheas are one of the few houseplants where the water in your tap genuinely matters.

Fluoride, chlorine and dissolved hardness minerals accumulate at the leaf margins as water evaporates, and burn the tissue. The result is the brown edge that people mistake for underwatering.

Use **filtered, distilled or rainwater**. Leaving tap water out overnight lets chlorine off-gas, but does nothing about fluoride or hard-water minerals — in a hard-water area it is not enough on its own.

Flush the pot with clean water every couple of months to leach out whatever has accumulated.

## Watering rhythm

Keep the soil **evenly moist** — damp like a wrung-out sponge, never soggy and never fully dry.

That usually means watering every five to seven days, checking with a finger an inch into the soil. If it feels dry at that depth, water. If it feels wet, wait a day and check again.

Both extremes cause visible damage. Dry soil produces curling and crisping; wet soil produces yellowing lower leaves and eventually root rot.

## Light

Medium, indirect, filtered. Think of the forest floor under a canopy.

- **Good:** a north-facing window, or several feet back from an east-facing one
- **Acceptable:** filtered light through a sheer curtain
- **Bad:** any direct sun, which fades the patterns and scorches leaves within days

Faded, washed-out markings almost always mean too much light. The colour will not return to damaged leaves, but new growth comes in vivid once you move it.

## The nightly movement

Calatheas belong to the prayer plant family, and they move. Leaves rise toward vertical in the evening and lower again in the morning, driven by cells at the base of each leaf stalk that swell and relax on a circadian rhythm.

This is normal and healthy — a plant that has stopped moving is often a stressed one. Do not confuse this vertical folding with the tight lengthwise curling that signals thirst.

## Dividing

Calatheas grow in clumps and are propagated only by division — cuttings do not root.

At repotting time in spring, tip the plant out and look for natural separations in the root mass. Ease the clump apart into sections, each with a good root system and at least three or four leaves. Pot each into fresh moisture-retentive mix.

Expect a sulk. Divisions droop and may drop a leaf or two while they establish. Keep them warm, humid and out of direct light for a few weeks, and they recover.

## If this sounds like too much

It is a fair conclusion. If you love the look but not the maintenance, **Maranta leuconeura** — the true prayer plant — offers similar patterned foliage with noticeably more tolerance for ordinary indoor humidity.
